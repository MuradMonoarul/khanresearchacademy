1. Course Description

Research Methodology Fundamentals: From Curiosity to Researchable Inquiry is an eight-week intensive introductory course designed to transform students from passive consumers of research knowledge into beginning researchers capable of thinking systematically about research problems. Unlike conventional research-methodology courses that often emphasize terminology and memorization, this course introduces research as a way of thinking, questioning, reasoning, evaluating evidence, and producing knowledge.

Participants move progressively from identifying an area of curiosity to understanding research paradigms, locating and critically reading scholarly literature, identifying research problems and gaps, developing research questions and objectives, understanding theory, selecting broad methodological approaches, considering sampling and ethics, and ultimately developing and defending an original research concept note. The course emphasizes learning by doing, peer learning, critical reflection, research integrity, responsible or ethical use of artificial intelligence, and continuous instructor feedback.

2. Course Philosophy

The central philosophy should be: Research cannot be learned only by learning about research. Research is learned by doing research. Therefore:

  • 30% — LEARN: Concepts, principles and examples
  • 40% — PRACTICE: Exercises, workshops and research labs
  • 20% — PRODUCE: Participants develop their own research ideas
  • 10% — REFLECT & RECEIVE FEEDBACK: Peer review, instructor feedback and revision

3. Overall Course Aim

To develop participants' foundational research literacy, critical inquiry and research-thinking competencies so that they can independently transform an area of interest into a coherent, theoretically informed, ethically responsible and methodologically feasible research concept.

4. Course Objectives

The course will enable participants to:

  • Develop an understanding of research as a systematic process of knowledge production.
  • Develop a research-oriented way of thinking based on curiosity, evidence and critical questioning.
  • Understand major philosophical assumptions and paradigms underlying research.
  • Distinguish among quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods approaches.
  • Transform broad interests into researchable problems.
  • Search for, evaluate, organize and synthesize scholarly literature.
  • Identify meaningful research gaps.
  • Formulate coherent research questions and objectives.
  • Understand the roles of concepts, variables, theory and conceptual frameworks.
  • Develop foundational understanding of research design and sampling.
  • Recognize major ethical issues and principles of research integrity.
  • Understand responsible and inappropriate uses of generative AI in research.
  • Integrate these competencies into a research concept note.
  • Communicate and defend a research idea academically.

5. Learning Outcomes

By successful completion, participants should be able to:

  • LO1 — Research Literacy: Explain the nature, purposes, characteristics and stages of systematic research.
  • LO2 — Research Thinking: Distinguish a general topic from a researchable problem and critically interrogate assumptions and evidence.
  • LO3 — Philosophical Understanding: Explain at an introductory level how ontology, epistemology and research paradigms influence methodological decisions.
  • LO4 — Literature Competency: Develop basic search strategies, locate credible academic literature, critically read research articles and organize literature systematically.
  • LO5 — Gap Identification: Distinguish several types of research gaps and formulate a defensible preliminary gap based on literature.
  • LO6 — Question Development: Develop logically aligned research problems, questions and objectives.
  • LO7 — Theoretical Thinking: Explain the role of concepts and theory and construct a preliminary conceptual representation of a research problem.
  • LO8 — Methodological Reasoning: Distinguish quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods approaches and justify a broad methodological direction appropriate to a question.
  • LO9 — Ethical Research: Recognize fundamental ethical and integrity issues in research.
  • LO10 — Research Communication: Develop, present and defend a coherent research concept note.

7. WEEK 1 — BECOMING A RESEARCHER

Theme: What Is Research and Why Do We Do It?

Session 1 - Research Beyond the Textbook

Content:

  • What is research?
  • Research versus information gathering
  • Research versus common sense
  • Scientific and systematic inquiry
  • Characteristics of rigorous research
  • Basic versus applied research
  • Exploratory, descriptive and explanatory purposes
  • Academic versus policy/applied research
  • Research as knowledge production
  • What researchers actually do

Session 2 - The Research Journey

Content: Research curiosity → problem → literature → gap → question → theory → methodology → evidence → interpretation → dissemination.

Introduce: Research process, Research cycle, Iteration, Uncertainty, Critical thinking, Researcher's mindset.

Research Lab / ACTIVITY

Example: Give students statements such as: "Social media is harmful to students."

Ask: Is this a research finding, assumption, opinion or hypothesis?

Then ask: What evidence would you need before accepting it?

This begins developing research thinking.

8. WEEK 2 — HOW RESEARCHERS KNOW

Theme: Paradigms, Evidence, and Ways of Knowing

Session 3: How Do We Know What We Know?

Introduce simply: Use everyday examples before academic definitions.

  • Ontology
  • Epistemology
  • Methodology
  • Methods

Session 4 - Major Research Paradigms

Introduce: Positivism, Postpositivism, Interpretivism, Constructivism, Critical approaches, Pragmatism.

Then demonstrate: Research Question → Philosophical Position → Methodological Approach → Methods

[Be careful to avoid turning this into an advanced philosophy course.]

Research Lab / Activity

Give one phenomenon: "University students' experiences with generative AI."

Different groups develop questions from:

  • Positivist/postpositivist
  • Constructivist
  • Critical
  • Pragmatic perspectives

Students discover that the same phenomenon can generate very different research questions.

9. WEEK 3 — FROM INTEREST TO RESEARCH PROBLEM

Theme: Finding Something Worth Investigating

Session 5 - Topic ≠ Research Problem

Teach: Area → Topic → Issue → Problem → Research problem → Research question

Stage Example

TopicGig work

ProblemOccupational insecurity among platform workers
Potential research problemLimited understanding of how algorithmic management shapes perceived employment insecurity among food-delivery workers.

Session 6: Building a Problem Statement

Cover: Context, Magnitude/nature of problem, Existing knowledge, Missing knowledge, Consequences, Research justification, Feasibility, Originality, Social/scientific significance.

Research Lab / Activity

Problem Tree: Participants develop: Causes → Core Problem → Consequences; Then transform the problem tree into researchable language.

10. WEEK 4 — ENTERING THE SCHOLARLY CONVERSATION

Theme: Finding and Reading Research Literature

Session 7: How Researchers Search

Teach: Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, discipline-specific databases, Keywords, Synonyms, Boolean operators, Phrase searching, Truncation, Inclusion logic, Citation chasing, Finding seminal versus recent literature.

Session 8 - How Researchers Read

This is crucial. Teach participants not to read every paper word-for-word initially.

The Research Article Anatomy: Title → Abstract → Introduction → Literature → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion

Teach them to extract: Question → Theory → Method → Sample → Findings → Limitations → Contribution

Research Lab

Participants search for papers related to their own topic and create a Literature Matrix:

CitationQuestionContextTheoryMethodSampleFindingsLimitationsRelevance
...........................

Introduce reference-management software such as Zotero.

11. WEEK 5 — FINDING THE GAP

Theme: Moving From "What We Know" to "What We Need to Know"

Session 9: What Is a Research Gap?

Challenge the common misconception: "There is no research in Bangladesh."

Introduce different gaps:

  • Evidence gap: Insufficient evidence.
  • Knowledge gap: Important aspects remain unknown.
  • Population gap: Certain populations are underrepresented.
  • Contextual gap: Existing evidence comes from different settings.
  • Methodological gap: Existing methods inadequately address the issue.
  • Theoretical gap: Existing theory insufficiently explains the phenomenon.
  • Contradictory evidence: Studies produce inconsistent findings.
  • Temporal gap: Changes make previous evidence potentially outdated.
  • Practice/policy gap: Evidence has not adequately addressed implementation.

Session 10 - From Literature to Argument

Teach Summary versus synthesis.

Bad (Shopping list style): Rahman found X. Ahmed found Y. Khan found Z.

Better: Existing studies consistently identify X; however, evidence concerning Y remains inconsistent, particularly among Z populations...

Research Lab

Students use their literature matrices to develop: Known → Unknown → Why Unknown Matters → Proposed Contribution

12. WEEK 6 — ASKING THE RIGHT QUESTION

Theme: Research Questions, Objectives, Concepts and Theory

Session 11: From Gap to Research Question

Characteristics of good research questions: Clear, Focused, Researchable, Feasible, Ethical, Significant, Aligned with gap.

Types: Descriptive, Exploratory, Explanatory, Comparative, Associational, Evaluative.

Introduce FINER/PICOT/SPIDER where appropriate without forcing one framework across disciplines.

Sequence: Question → General objective → Specific objectives.

Session 12 - Concepts, Variables and Theory

Introduce: Concept, Construct, Variable, Independent/dependent variable, Operationalization.

Then: Theory, Theoretical framework, Conceptual framework.

Emphasize: A theory is not something added to make a paper look academic. It provides a lens for understanding the phenomenon.

Research Lab

Each participant develops: Primary question, Secondary questions, General objective, Specific objectives, Key concepts, Preliminary conceptual map.

13. WEEK 7 — HOW COULD WE STUDY IT?

Theme: Introduction to Research Design, Sampling and Ethics

Session 13 - Choosing an Approach

Quantitative research, Qualitative research, Mixed methods.

Introduce common designs at a conceptual level: Survey, Cross-sectional, Longitudinal, Experimental/quasi-experimental, Case study, Phenomenology, Ethnography, Narrative research, Grounded theory.

The key lesson: Method follows question—not the other way around.

Session 14 - People, Sampling and Ethics

Introduce: Population, Sample, Sampling frame, Probability versus non-probability sampling.

Basic techniques: Random, Systematic, Stratified, Cluster, Purposive, Snowball, Convenience.

Research Ethics: Informed consent, Voluntariness, Confidentiality, Anonymity, Risk-benefit, Vulnerability, Power, Data protection.

Research Integrity: Plagiarism, Fabrication, Falsification, Authorship, Duplicate publication, Predatory journals, Citation manipulation.

Responsible AI:

  • Appropriate: Brainstorming, Language support, Explaining concepts, Coding assistance with safeguards, Search-term development.
  • Potentially problematic: Fabricated citations, Submitting AI output without verification, Generating fake data, Uploading confidential participant data, Using AI to conceal plagiarism.

14. WEEK 8 — FROM STUDENT TO BEGINNING RESEARCHER

Theme: Integrating and Defending the Research Idea

Session 15 - Writing a Research Concept Note

Bring everything together: Working title, Background, Problem, Preliminary literature, Research gap, Research question, Objectives, Conceptual/theoretical orientation, Proposed methodological direction, Significance, Ethical considerations, References.

Session 16 - Research Idea Defence

Each participant gets a 5–7-minute presentation followed by 5 minutes Q&A.

Questions might include:

  • What exactly is your research problem?
  • What evidence shows this problem matters?
  • Where is the gap?
  • How does your question address that gap?
  • Who would benefit from the research?
  • Why is your proposed methodological direction appropriate?
  • Is the study feasible?
  • What ethical concerns exist?

This should feel like a mini academic conference.

15. FINAL CAPSTONE

Research Concept Note: Suggested length of 2,500–3,000 words. It is intentionally not a full research proposal.

[Course 1 produces the concept. Course 2 turns the concept into a research-ready proposal and instruments.]

20. Feedback Model

A major differentiator should be feedback. Each important assignment gets:

  • Level 1 — Peer Feedback: Students learn by reviewing others.
  • Level 2 — Teaching Assistant Feedback: Technical feedback.
  • Level 3 — Faculty Feedback: For the research problem, questions and final concept note.

Use: Strength → Concern → Specific Recommendation rather than simply writing "good" or "needs improvement."

21. Weekly Research Clinic

A 60-minute optional Research Clinic each week or every second week. No lecture. Students arrive with problems (e.g., "I can't identify my gap," "My question is too broad"), and the instructor/TA troubleshoot live.

23. Recommended Core Reading

Avoid requiring students to purchase one expensive textbook. Use selected chapters/readings from reputable methodology texts such as:

  • Creswell & Creswell — Research Design
  • Bryman — Social Research Methods
  • Saunders, Lewis & Thornhill — Research Methods for Business Students
  • Booth et al. — The Craft of Research
  • Patton — Qualitative Research & Evaluation Methods
  • Plus discipline-specific journal articles.

24. AI Policy

Every participant should sign/accept a simple Responsible AI for Research Agreement.

AI may support learning, brainstorming and language refinement, subject to instructor rules. Students remain responsible for accuracy, sources, citations, originality, interpretation, and academic integrity.

Prohibited: Fabricating data, fabricating citations, submitting unverified AI-generated content, uploading confidential participant information, or using AI to circumvent assignments.
